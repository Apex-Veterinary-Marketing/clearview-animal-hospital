Heartworm disease is one of those illnesses many pet owners think could never happen to their pet.

“My dog barely goes outside.”

“My cat lives indoors.”

“We live in a closed community.”

“We don’t see many mosquitoes.”

Unfortunately, none of those situations completely eliminate the risk.

Any dog or cat exposed to a mosquito can potentially be exposed to heartworms — including indoor pets and pets living in apartments, gated neighborhoods, or other closed communities.

That’s why heartworm prevention and routine heartworm testing are such important parts of your pet’s preventive healthcare.

## What Is Heartworm Disease?

Heartworm disease is caused by a parasite called Dirofilaria immitis. It is spread through the bite of an infected mosquito.

A mosquito picks up immature heartworm larvae after biting an infected animal. When that mosquito later bites another dog or cat, it can transmit those larvae.

Over time, the larvae can develop into adult heartworms that affect the heart, lungs, and surrounding blood vessels.

Because mosquitoes transmit heartworms, your pet does not need to have contact with another infected animal to become infected.

## “But My Pet Lives Indoors!”

Indoor pets still need heartworm protection.

Mosquitoes can enter homes through:

- Open doors

- Windows

- Screens

- Garages

- Porches

- Small openings around the home

Your dog may only spend a few minutes outside for bathroom breaks, and your cat may never intentionally leave the house. Unfortunately, a mosquito only needs one opportunity to bite.

Indoor living can reduce certain risks, but it does not make a pet immune to heartworm disease.

## What About Apartments, Gated Neighborhoods, and Closed Communities?

Living in a controlled or closed environment doesn’t eliminate heartworm risk either.

Mosquitoes do not recognize property lines, gates, fences, or neighborhood boundaries.

They can travel into apartment complexes, gated communities, neighborhoods, boarding facilities, and other areas where pets may have limited interaction with animals outside their immediate environment.

The important thing to remember is simple:

Heartworms are spread by mosquitoes — not by direct contact between pets.

Your pet does not need to visit a dog park, interact with stray animals, or live near an obviously infected animal to be at risk.

## Why Is Heartworm Testing So Important?

Heartworm disease can develop quietly.

During the earlier stages of infection, a dog may appear completely normal. By the time obvious symptoms develop, the disease may already be affecting the heart and lungs.

Routine heartworm testing for dogs allows your veterinarian to identify an infection before serious disease progresses.

Testing is also important for pets taking heartworm prevention. Although today’s preventive medications are highly effective when given correctly, missed or late doses, vomiting medication, incorrect dosing, or other circumstances can potentially leave a pet unprotected.

Regular testing provides another important layer of protection.

## What Are the Signs of Heartworm Disease in Dogs?

Dogs with heartworm disease may eventually develop symptoms such as:

- Coughing

- Exercise intolerance

- Becoming tired more easily

- Decreased appetite

- Weight loss

- Difficulty breathing

- Weakness

- Abdominal swelling

- Collapse in severe cases

One of the challenges of heartworm disease is that your dog may have no obvious symptoms during the early stages.

That’s one reason preventive testing matters.

## What About Heartworms in Cats?

Cats can develop heartworm disease too, including cats that live entirely indoors.

Heartworm disease behaves differently in cats than it does in dogs. Cats often have fewer adult worms, but even a small number of worms can cause significant respiratory illness.

Signs in cats can include:

- Coughing

- Wheezing

- Vomiting

- Difficulty breathing

- Decreased appetite

- Weight loss

- Lethargy

- Sudden collapse

Heartworm testing in cats can also be more complicated than testing in dogs. Depending on your cat’s health, symptoms, and risk factors, your veterinarian may recommend a combination of testing methods.

Because there is no approved medication to eliminate adult heartworms in cats in the same way heartworm disease is treated in dogs, prevention is especially important for cats.

## Why Test If My Dog Is Already on Heartworm Prevention?

This is a great question — and one we hear frequently.

Heartworm preventives are extremely effective when administered correctly and on schedule. However, real life happens.

A dose may be:

- Forgotten

- Given late

- Spit out

- Vomited

- Incorrectly administered

- Missed during a refill delay

Routine testing helps confirm that your dog remains heartworm-negative and allows an infection to be identified as early as possible if something unexpected occurs. Prevention and testing work together.

## Heartworm Disease Is Much Easier to Prevent Than Treat

Treating heartworm disease in dogs can be lengthy, expensive, and physically demanding for both the pet and the family.

Treatment may involve multiple medications, injections, diagnostic tests, strict exercise restriction, and careful monitoring.Prevention is considerably simpler.

Year-round heartworm prevention for dogs and cats helps stop immature heartworms from developing into adult parasites.

Keeping prevention current and following your veterinarian’s recommended testing schedule are two of the most important things you can do to protect your pet.

## Don’t Let the Seasons Fool You

It can be tempting to think heartworm prevention is only necessary during the hottest months of the year.

Mosquito activity, however, can vary significantly with weather and climate. Warm periods can occur outside the traditional summer months, and mosquitoes may survive in protected indoor environments.

For this reason, veterinarians commonly recommend year-round heartworm prevention rather than trying to predict when mosquito exposure will occur.

## The Bottom Line: If Mosquitoes Can Reach Your Pet, Heartworms Can Too

Whether your pet:Lives indoors. Lives in an apartment. Lives in a gated or closed community. Only goes outside for a few minutes. Rarely sees other animals.

Routine heartworm testing, annual wellness examinations, and year-round heartworm prevention can help protect your pet from a serious and potentially life-threatening disease.
