      <h2>Pet Ultrasound Near Easley, SC</h2><p>When your pet isn’t feeling well, the answer is often hidden inside — in the stomach, intestines, liver, kidneys, bladder or heart. Ultrasound lets us look at those organs in real time, without surgery and without radiation.</p><p>At Clearview Animal Hospital, ultrasound is one of the diagnostic tools we use alongside our in-house laboratory and digital radiography. Ultrasound uses sound waves to create a moving picture of the soft tissues inside your pet’s body, showing the size, shape and texture of organs and revealing things like fluid, stones, cysts or masses. Seeing what’s going on inside helps us understand your pet’s symptoms, decide on the right treatment sooner, and avoid guesswork — which means less worry for you and a quicker path to feeling better for your pet.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect During Your Pet’s Ultrasound</h2><p>An ultrasound exam is painless and non-invasive. Your pet lies comfortably on a padded table, usually on their back or side, while a team member gently helps them stay still. Most pets don’t need sedation, although a mild sedative can help a pet who is very nervous or wiggly.</p><p>Because sound waves don’t travel well through air, a small patch of fur over the area being examined is usually shaved, and a water-based gel is applied to the skin. We then move a small handheld probe over the area and watch the images on screen as we go. We may ask you not to feed your pet beforehand, since a full stomach can make some organs harder to see.</p><p>Because the images appear live, we can often share initial findings with you right away. We’ll explain what we saw, how it fits with your pet’s other test results, and what we recommend next.</p>

      <h2>When Your Pet May Need an Ultrasound</h2><p>We may recommend an ultrasound if your pet has ongoing vomiting or diarrhea, a swollen or painful belly, blood in the urine or trouble urinating, unexplained weight loss, abnormal bloodwork, a suspected mass, or signs that could involve the heart. It’s also useful for taking a closer look at something found on X-rays or during an exam.</p><h2>When Should Your Pet Have an Ultrasound?</h2><p>Ultrasound isn’t a routine yearly test — it’s used when there’s a specific question to answer. Some pets need a single scan, while pets with ongoing conditions may have repeat scans to track changes or response to treatment. We’ll recommend an ultrasound when it’s likely to give useful answers for your pet.</p><h2>Practice Highlights</h2><p>What Clearview brings to your pet’s diagnostic care:</p><ul><li><strong>Ultrasound</strong> — A painless look at your pet’s internal organs, right here in Easley.</li><li><strong>Digital radiography</strong> — X-rays and ultrasound work together to give a fuller picture.</li><li><strong>In-house laboratory</strong> — Lab results help us interpret what we see on the scan.</li><li><strong>Payment options</strong> — CareCredit and Cherry can help you plan for diagnostic care.</li></ul>

faqs:
  - question: "Does an ultrasound hurt my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>No. Ultrasound is a non-invasive, painless exam that uses sound waves rather than radiation, and most pets lie still comfortably. Sedation usually isn’t needed, though a sedative may be used for a very frightened or restless pet, and biopsies require sedation or a short anesthetic.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ultrasound-examination-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Ultrasound Examination in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "Why does my pet’s fur need to be shaved?"
    answer: |-
      <p>The ultrasound probe needs full contact with the skin, and sound waves don’t travel through the air trapped in fur. Shaving a small area gives the clearest images. The fur grows back.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ultrasound-examination-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Ultrasound Examination in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "What can an ultrasound show?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Ultrasound shows the size, shape and texture of organs such as the liver, kidneys and bladder, and it can evaluate the heart’s walls, chambers, valves and blood flow. It can also help find cysts and tumors, confirm pregnancy, and guide a needle when a biopsy is needed.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ultrasound-examination-in-cats">VCA Animal Hospitals, Ultrasound Examination in Cats</a></p>
  - question: "How is an ultrasound different from an X-ray?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Ultrasound uses sound waves instead of radiation and shows soft tissues as a live, moving picture. Air and bone block the sound waves, though, so ultrasound isn’t useful for looking at normal lungs, bones, the brain or the spinal cord. That’s why ultrasound and X-rays are often used together, each answering different questions.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ultrasound-examination-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Ultrasound Examination in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "How soon will I know the results?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Ultrasound images are viewed in real time, so many findings are known during the exam. If images are sent to a specialist for review, a final written report can take a few days.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ultrasound-examination-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Ultrasound Examination in Dogs</a></p>
