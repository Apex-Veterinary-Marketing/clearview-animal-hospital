      <h2>FIV and FeLV Testing for Cats Near Easley, SC</h2><p>Feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV) and feline leukemia virus (FeLV) are two of the most common serious infections in cats. Both can weaken a cat’s immune system, and FeLV is also linked to cancer and blood disorders. The tricky part is that infected cats often look perfectly healthy for months or even years — so testing is the only way to know your cat’s status.</p><p>At Clearview Animal Hospital, FIV/FeLV testing is one of the tests handled through our <a href="/services/laboratory">in-house laboratory</a> services. Knowing whether your cat carries either virus helps you protect the other cats in your home, helps us interpret symptoms if your cat gets sick, and lets us tailor your cat’s preventive care to the risks they actually face.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect at Your Cat’s FIV/FeLV Test</h2><p>The test starts with a small blood sample, which most cats handle well. We’ll ask about your cat’s history — where they came from, whether they go outdoors, whether they live with other cats, and whether they’ve had any fights or bite wounds — because that background helps us read the results correctly.</p><p>A negative result is reassuring, but it may not be the final word if your cat was exposed very recently, since it can take time for the infection to show up on a test. A positive result in a cat that seems healthy is usually confirmed with follow-up testing before any conclusions are drawn. Kittens can also test positive for FIV because of antibodies passed on from their mother, so young kittens may need to be retested as they grow.</p><p>Whatever the results, we’ll explain what they mean in plain language and talk with you about next steps, from retesting to keeping your cat — and the other cats in your home — as healthy as possible.</p>

      <h2>Which Cats Should Be Tested?</h2><p>We recommend FIV/FeLV testing for every new kitten or cat before they join a household with other cats, for cats who become sick, and for cats who may have been exposed — for example, after a fight or a bite wound, or when another cat in the home tests positive. Cats who go outdoors, live with cats of unknown status, or were born to an infected mother are at higher risk.</p><h2>How Often Should Your Cat Be Tested?</h2><p>Every cat should have their FIV and FeLV status checked when they first come into your home and again if they become ill. Cats with an ongoing risk of exposure, such as cats who spend time outdoors, should be retested regularly, and any cat with a possible recent exposure may need a repeat test after a waiting period. faqs:
  - question: "How do cats get FIV?"
    answer: |-
      <p>FIV spreads mainly through bite wounds from an infected cat. Casual contact, such as sharing water bowls or grooming each other, doesn’t appear to spread it efficiently, so cats in calm households where housemates don’t fight are at low risk. Unneutered male cats with outdoor access are at the greatest risk because they’re more likely to fight.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-immunodeficiency-virus">Cornell Feline Health Center, Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)</a></p>
  - question: "How is FeLV spread?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Cats infected with FeLV shed the virus in saliva, as well as in urine, feces and milk. Any close contact can spread it, including bite wounds, grooming each other and sharing food dishes or litter boxes, and a mother cat can pass it to her kittens. Kittens are at greater risk than adult cats because their immune systems are still developing.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-leukemia-virus">Cornell Feline Health Center, Feline Leukemia Virus</a></p>
  - question: "My cat tested negative for FIV. Why might we test again?"
    answer: |-
      <p>FIV tests look for antibodies to the virus, and it can take two to six months after infection for a cat to make enough antibodies to be detected. A cat infected very recently can therefore test negative. If an exposure is possible, retesting after at least 60 days gives a more accurate result.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-immunodeficiency-virus">Cornell Feline Health Center, Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)</a></p>
  - question: "Why does a positive FeLV test need follow-up testing?"
    answer: |-
      <p>False positives are possible with FeLV screening tests, so confirmatory testing is recommended after a positive result. A cat’s body can respond to FeLV in different ways, and no single test at one point in time can tell whether an infection is progressive. Follow-up testing several weeks later helps show how the infection is behaving.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-leukemia-virus">Cornell Feline Health Center, Feline Leukemia Virus</a></p>
  - question: "Will the FeLV vaccine affect my cat’s test results?"
    answer: |-
      <p>No. FeLV vaccines don’t cause false positive results on FeLV tests. The vaccine doesn’t protect every cat, though, so testing before bringing a new cat home and limiting contact with infected cats are still important. It’s considered a core vaccine for kittens and is recommended for cats at risk of exposure, such as indoor/outdoor cats.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-leukemia-virus">Cornell Feline Health Center, Feline Leukemia Virus</a></p>
