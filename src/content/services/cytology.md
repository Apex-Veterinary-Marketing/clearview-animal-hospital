      <h2>Pet Cytology Near Easley, SC</h2><p>When your dog or cat has a new lump, a patch of irritated skin or an ear that keeps bothering them, the first question is always the same: what is it? Cytology is often one of the quickest ways to start finding out.</p><p>Cytology is the examination of cells under a microscope. A small sample of cells is collected — from a lump, the skin, the ears or a body fluid — and studied for clues such as bacteria, yeast, inflammation or abnormal cells. At Clearview Animal Hospital, cytology is one of the diagnostic tests offered through our <a href="/services/laboratory">in-house laboratory</a> services. Depending on the sample, it may be examined here or sent to a veterinary pathologist for review.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect When Your Pet Has Cytology</h2><p>It starts with an exam, so we can decide where and how to collect the sample. For a lump, the most common method is a fine needle aspirate: a very small needle is placed into the lump to collect a few cells. For skin or ears, we may gently swab the area, press a glass slide against a moist sore or lightly scrape the skin’s surface. Most pets tolerate this well, and many samples can be collected without sedation.</p><p>The cells are placed on a slide, stained and examined under a microscope. Cytology can often tell whether a problem is caused by inflammation or infection, and whether a lump looks like a benign growth or something more concerning.</p><p>Cytology looks at individual cells, while a <a href="/services/biopsies">biopsy</a> removes a piece of tissue so a pathologist can see how the cells are arranged. If the cytology results aren’t conclusive, or a lump needs a more definite answer, we may recommend a biopsy as the next step. Either way, we’ll go over the results with you and explain what we recommend.</p>

faqs:
  - question: "What can cytology tell us about my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Cytology is the microscopic examination of cells collected from the body. The most important thing it can show is whether a problem is caused by inflammation or by abnormal tissue growth. When there’s inflammation, it can often point to a cause such as bacteria, parasites or fungus, and when a growth is found, it can usually show what type of tissue is involved and whether it looks benign or malignant.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/cytology">VCA Animal Hospitals, Cytology: General</a></p>
  - question: "How are the cells collected?"
    answer: |-
      <p>The most common method is fine needle aspiration, which uses a very thin needle and a syringe to draw cells out of a lump or organ. Other methods include scraping the surface of the skin, pressing a glass slide onto an oozing sore, using a cotton swab on moist areas, and washing an internal surface with sterile fluid to collect cells.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/cytology">VCA Animal Hospitals, Cytology: General</a></p>
  - question: "Does my pet need to be sedated for cytology?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Often not. Cytology is a simple, quick, relatively painless and non-invasive way to gather information, and samples can often be collected without sedation or anesthesia. Your veterinarian will let you know if sedation would help keep your pet comfortable for a particular sample.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/cytology">VCA Animal Hospitals, Cytology: General</a></p>
  - question: "My dog’s lump feels soft. Why sample it?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Soft, movable lumps are often lipomas (fatty tumors), but other tumors, such as mast cell tumors, can look and feel similar. A fine needle aspirate is needed to rule those out. Even benign lipomas can grow, so they’re worth keeping an eye on.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/skin-disorders-of-dogs/tumors-of-the-skin-in-dogs">Merck Veterinary Manual, Tumors of the Skin in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "What happens if cytology doesn’t give a clear answer?"
    answer: |-
      <p>The cells collected may not show everything happening in the tissue — the sample can be small, or the most telling cells may be missed. When cytology isn’t definitive, the next step is usually histology: examining a small piece of tissue collected surgically, which shows how the cells are organized and is generally considered the most definitive test.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/cytology">VCA Animal Hospitals, Cytology: General</a></p>
