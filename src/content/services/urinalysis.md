      <h2>Pet Urinalysis Near Easley, SC</h2><p>Accidents in the house, straining in the litter box, drinking more water than usual — changes in your pet’s bathroom habits are worth paying attention to. A urine test is one of the simplest ways to find out what’s behind them.</p><p>A urinalysis looks at your pet’s urine in several ways: its color and clarity, how concentrated it is, its chemistry (such as pH, protein and glucose) and what’s in it under the microscope, like cells, crystals and bacteria. At Clearview Animal Hospital, urinalysis is one of the tests handled by our <a href="/services/laboratory">in-house laboratory</a>. The results tell us how well the kidneys are working and whether there are signs of infection, inflammation, bladder stones or crystals, and they can also point to problems elsewhere in the body, such as diabetes or liver disease.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect When Your Pet Has a Urinalysis</h2><p>First, we’ll examine your pet and ask about any changes you’ve noticed, such as how often your pet is urinating, how much they’re drinking, and whether you’ve seen blood or straining. Then we’ll collect a urine sample in the way that best fits your pet and the reason for the test.</p><p>Sometimes a sample caught while your pet urinates normally is all we need, and we may ask you to bring one from home. For other tests, especially when we’re checking for a bladder infection, a cleaner sample collected directly from the bladder with a small needle (cystocentesis) gives more reliable results. It’s quick, and most pets tolerate it well. Because urine changes as it sits, a fresh sample gives the most accurate picture, so we’ll let you know how and when to collect it.</p><p>Once the results are in, we’ll explain what they mean, what we recommend next and whether any follow-up testing, such as bloodwork or a urine culture, would help.</p>

faqs:
  - question: "What does a urinalysis check?"
    answer: |-
      <p>A urinalysis has four parts: the urine’s appearance (color and cloudiness), its concentration (specific gravity), its pH and chemistry, including glucose, and a microscopic look at the sediment for cells, crystals and bacteria. It helps assess the kidneys and urinary tract and can also point to problems elsewhere, such as diabetes, liver disease or metabolic disorders.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/urinalysis">VCA Animal Hospitals, Urinalysis</a></p>
  - question: "How do you collect a urine sample from my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>There are three common methods. Cystocentesis uses a needle passed through the belly wall into the bladder and gives a relatively clean sample that is good for detecting bacteria. A catheter can be passed through the urethra into the bladder, though sedation is often needed. A midstream “free catch” is collected as your pet urinates normally; it’s noninvasive and can be done at home, but it’s more likely to contain bacteria or debris from outside the bladder.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/urinalysis">VCA Animal Hospitals, Urinalysis</a></p>
  - question: "Why does the urine sample need to be fresh?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Urine can change after it is collected. Crystals, for example, can form in the sample itself, especially when testing is delayed, which can make the results harder to interpret. That’s why your veterinarian may examine a fresh sample soon after it is collected.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/urinalysis">VCA Animal Hospitals, Urinalysis</a></p>
  - question: "What are the signs of a urinary problem in cats?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Cats with lower urinary tract disease may strain or seem painful when urinating, go more often, cry out while urinating, have blood in the urine, urinate outside the litter box or lick the genital area repeatedly. A urinalysis is usually part of the first workup. A urethral obstruction — more common in male cats — is an absolute emergency that needs immediate veterinary care.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-lower-urinary-tract-disease">Cornell Feline Health Center, Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease</a></p>
  - question: "Can my dog have a bladder infection without showing signs?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Yes. A bacterial bladder infection can cause frequent or painful urination, accidents in the house and sometimes blood in the urine, but some dogs show no signs at all — especially dogs with conditions such as diabetes, kidney disease or excess steroid hormone production. Your veterinarian diagnoses an infection by testing a urine sample and culturing it to identify the bacteria.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/kidney-and-urinary-tract-disorders-of-dogs/infectious-diseases-of-the-urinary-system-in-dogs">Merck Veterinary Manual, Infectious Diseases of the Urinary System in Dogs</a></p>
