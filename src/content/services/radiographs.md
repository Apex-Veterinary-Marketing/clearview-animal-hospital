      <h2>Digital Radiographs for Pets Near Easley, SC</h2><p>When your dog is limping, your cat is coughing, or your pet may have swallowed something they shouldn’t have, an exam can only tell us so much from the outside. Radiographs — better known as X-rays — let us see inside your pet’s body and find answers quickly.</p><p>At Clearview Animal Hospital, radiographs are one of the tests we handle right here, using digital radiography. X-rays show bones and joints especially well, along with the outline of the heart, lungs and organs in the belly. They help us look for broken bones, arthritis and other joint problems, swallowed objects, bladder stones, fluid in the chest and changes in the size or shape of organs. Because the images are digital, they’re ready to review quickly, and we’ll talk you through what we see.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect When Your Pet Has X-Rays</h2><p>It starts with an exam. We’ll check your pet, ask about their symptoms and decide which area of the body to image. Most of the time, we take more than one view — for example, from the side and from above — so we can see the area from different angles.</p><p>Taking radiographs is painless. Your pet simply needs to lie still in the right position for a few moments while each image is taken. Your pet is gently positioned for each view, and protective covers and shields are used to limit exposure. If your pet is painful, anxious or wiggly, a sedative may be recommended to keep them relaxed and help us get clear images. For safety, owners usually aren’t in the room while X-rays are taken.</p><p>Afterward, we’ll go over the images with you and explain what they show. Radiographs are often used together with our <a href="/services/laboratory">in-house laboratory</a> and ultrasound to build a complete picture of your pet’s health and plan the next steps.</p>

Radiographs can also help check dental problems below the gumline and track healing after an injury or orthopedic surgery.</p><h2>How Often Should Your Pet Have X-Rays?</h2><p>Radiographs aren’t a routine yearly test — they’re taken when there’s a specific question to answer. Some conditions call for follow-up images, such as checking how a fracture is healing or monitoring a heart, lung or joint condition over time. faqs:
  - question: "What can X-rays show in my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Radiographs show bones and joints well, including many bone abnormalities. They also show the outline of the heart and large blood vessels, fluid in the lungs, foreign objects or gas in the intestines, the size and shape of organs like the liver, kidneys and spleen, and dental problems beneath the gums.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/radiographs-for-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Radiographs (X-Rays) for Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "Do X-rays hurt? Will my pet need to be sedated?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Taking X-rays is painless. Some pets are sedated to reduce stress and help them stay still, which helps produce clear images. Your veterinarian will let you know whether sedation is a good idea for your pet.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.merckvetmanual.com/special-pet-topics/diagnostic-tests-and-imaging/diagnostic-imaging">Merck Veterinary Manual, Diagnostic Imaging</a></p>
  - question: "Are X-rays safe for my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>X-ray exposure is very brief and uses a low dose of radiation. Lead shields help protect the rest of the body as well as the people taking the images, which is also why owners usually wait outside while the images are taken.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.merckvetmanual.com/special-pet-topics/diagnostic-tests-and-imaging/diagnostic-imaging">Merck Veterinary Manual, Diagnostic Imaging</a></p>
  - question: "What’s the difference between X-rays and ultrasound?"
    answer: |-
      <p>X-rays are good for bones and large body cavities and for finding fractures, tumors, injuries and deformities, but soft tissues don’t show as well. Ultrasound is better for showing soft tissues, but it can’t image the lungs, intestines or bone well. That’s why the two are often used together.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.merckvetmanual.com/special-pet-topics/diagnostic-tests-and-imaging/diagnostic-imaging">Merck Veterinary Manual, Diagnostic Imaging</a></p>
  - question: "Is there anything X-rays can’t show?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Yes. Standard radiographs don’t show the inside of the skull, including the brain, or fine internal structures such as the inside of the heart and bladder very well. Some organs can also be harder to see in very thin or very overweight pets. In these cases, other imaging such as ultrasound, CT or MRI may be recommended.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/radiographs-for-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Radiographs (X-Rays) for Dogs</a></p>
