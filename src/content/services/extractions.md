      <h2>Relief From Painful Teeth for Pets in Easley, SC</h2><p>Nobody likes the idea of a pet losing a tooth, but sometimes removing one is the kindest choice. A tooth that is badly infected, broken or loose can cause constant pain — and because dogs and cats are so good at hiding discomfort, many keep eating and acting normally while they quietly suffer.</p><p>At Clearview Animal Hospital, extractions are part of our dental care. When a tooth can’t be saved, removing it takes away the source of pain and infection and lets the gums heal. Many families are surprised by how much brighter and more playful their pet becomes once a sore tooth is gone. Pets also adapt remarkably well to missing teeth and usually go right on enjoying their meals.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect When Your Pet Needs an Extraction</h2><p>Extractions are done while your pet is under general anesthesia, usually as part of a dental cleaning. Before anything is removed, digital radiographs (dental X-rays) show us the roots and the bone around them, so we can see exactly which teeth are diseased and plan the safest way to remove them.</p><p>Some teeth come out easily; others have long or multiple roots and need more care to remove completely. Leaving a piece of root behind can lead to ongoing pain or infection, which is why the X-rays matter so much. Once the tooth is out, the area is cleaned and the gum is often closed with dissolvable stitches.</p><p>When your pet goes home, we’ll explain what was done and give you clear aftercare instructions, including what to feed while the mouth heals and any medications your pet needs. If anything seems off during recovery, call us.</p>

      <h2>Signs Your Pet May Have a Painful Tooth</h2><p>Extractions are most often needed for advanced periodontal disease, broken teeth, painful resorptive lesions in cats, and baby teeth that never fell out. Watch for bad breath, drooling, bleeding from the mouth, chewing on one side, dropping food, pawing at the face, a swollen cheek, or a pet who suddenly prefers soft food. Any of these deserve a dental exam.</p><h2>When Should Your Pet Have a Tooth Extracted?</h2><p>An extraction is a one-time treatment for a tooth that can’t be saved, and it’s best done sooner rather than later so your pet isn’t living with pain. The need is usually found during a dental exam or cleaning, when X-rays show the full story. Regular dental checkups and home care help keep the rest of your pet’s teeth healthy.</p><h2>Practice Highlights</h2><p>Why families trust Clearview with their pet’s dental care:</p><ul><li><strong>Digital radiography</strong> — Dental X-rays guide every decision about which teeth need to come out.</li><li><strong>Thorough cleanings</strong> — Ultrasonic scaling and polishing for the teeth that stay.</li><li><strong>Caring for Easley since 1958</strong> — A trusted local animal hospital for generations of Upstate pets.</li><li><strong>Payment options</strong> — CareCredit and Cherry can help make dental care more manageable.</li></ul>

For teeth like these, extraction is often the most effective way to relieve pain and stop infection.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dental-disease-in-cats">VCA Animal Hospitals, Dental Disease in Cats</a></p>
  - question: "My dog broke a tooth. Does it have to come out?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Not always, but it does need treatment. When a fracture exposes the pulp (the nerve), bacteria can infect the tooth and the bone around it, causing pain. Treatment options include root canal therapy or extraction, and ignoring the problem isn’t a good option because the tooth will become painful.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/fractured-teeth-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Fractured Teeth in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "What is tooth resorption in cats?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Tooth resorption is a slow breakdown of the tooth structure that creates holes in the crown and root. Once the sensitive inner layers are exposed, it becomes very painful. Dental X-rays are used to decide whether the whole tooth must be removed or whether removing the crown is enough.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/tooth-resorption-in-cats">VCA Animal Hospitals, Tooth Resorption in Cats</a></p>
  - question: "My puppy still has baby teeth next to the adult teeth. Is that a problem?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Yes. Baby teeth that don’t fall out can trap food and push adult teeth out of position, leading to dental disease and injuries to the mouth. Small and short-nosed breeds are especially prone. Unless the baby tooth is very loose, extraction is usually recommended, and removing it early can let the adult tooth move into the right place.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/pediatric/puppy/health-wellness/why-does-puppy-still-have-baby-teeth">VCA Animal Hospitals, Why Does My Puppy Still Have Baby Teeth?</a></p>
  - question: "Is my pet checked before anesthesia for an extraction?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Pre-anesthetic bloodwork is usually recommended to check organs such as the liver and kidneys and to confirm your pet is a good candidate for anesthesia. If results are abnormal, the plan can be adjusted or the procedure postponed until the problem is addressed.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/bloodwork">VCA Animal Hospitals, Preanesthetic Bloodwork</a></p>
