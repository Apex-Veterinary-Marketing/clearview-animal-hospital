      <h2>What to Expect From a Nutrition Consultation</h2><p>Nutrition conversations usually happen as part of your pet’s exam. We’ll weigh your pet, assess their body condition, and ask what they eat in a typical day — their main food, treats, table scraps and any supplements. It helps to bring a photo of the food label or a list of everything your pet eats.</p><p>From there, we’ll look at the whole picture: your pet’s age, breed, activity level, allergies and medical history. If your pet needs to lose or gain weight, we’ll suggest a realistic feeding plan and how much to feed. If your pet has a health concern such as digestive upset, heart health or urinary problems, we may recommend a diet designed to support that condition. We’ll also help you make sense of food labels so you can shop with confidence. Follow-up weigh-ins let us see what’s working and fine-tune the plan as your pet’s needs change.</p>

faqs:
  - question: "How can I tell if my pet is at a healthy weight?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Veterinarians use a body condition score, which looks at how easily you can feel your pet’s ribs, whether they have a visible waist, and how their belly tucks up. On the common 9-point scale, a score of 4 or 5 is considered ideal. Ask us at your next visit and we’ll walk you through checking your pet at home.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/body-condition-scores">VCA Animal Hospitals, Body Condition Scoring in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "Is a few extra pounds really a problem?"
    answer: |-
      <p>It can be. Excess weight raises the risk of arthritis and joint pain, diabetes, heart disease and other chronic conditions, and it can shorten a dog’s life. The good news is that weight gain can be reversed with the right diet and activity plan.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/obesity-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Obesity in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "Which people foods are unsafe for pets?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Several common foods can be harmful, including chocolate, coffee and caffeine, grapes and raisins, onions, garlic and chives, macadamia nuts, alcohol, raw yeast dough, and xylitol, a sweetener found in gum, candy, baked goods and toothpaste. If your pet eats something on this list, call us or an animal poison control center right away.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets">ASPCA, People Foods to Avoid Feeding Your Pets</a></p>
  - question: "Can I make my pet’s food at home?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Home-cooked diets can work, but recipes that aren’t carefully formulated often fall short on key nutrients such as calcium, phosphorus and zinc, and swapping ingredients can throw a recipe off balance. If you’d like to feed a homemade diet, the safest route is a recipe designed by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist, and we can help you find one.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/nutrition---home-made-diets">VCA Animal Hospitals, Home-Made Diets</a></p>
  - question: "How do I choose a good commercial pet food?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Look beyond the ingredient list. The World Small Animal Veterinary Association recommends using the information on the label — such as whether the food is complete and balanced for your pet’s life stage — and talking with your veterinary team about which food fits your pet’s needs. Its “Selecting a Pet Food” guide walks through the questions to ask.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://wsava.org/global-guidelines/global-nutrition-guidelines/">World Small Animal Veterinary Association, Global Nutrition Guidelines</a></p>
