      <h2>What to Expect When Your Pet Has Surgery</h2><p>Surgery starts well before the procedure. A pre-operative evaluation — an exam and, in most cases, bloodwork from our in-house laboratory — helps us make sure your pet is ready for anesthesia and lets us tailor the plan to your pet. We’ll explain the procedure, answer your questions and give you instructions for the night before, such as when to stop feeding.</p><p>On the day of surgery, your pet is placed under general anesthesia so they feel no pain during the procedure. Your pet is closely watched through surgery and while waking up, and we’ll go over discharge instructions with you when it’s time to go home.</p><p>Recovery at home matters just as much. You’ll learn how to keep the incision clean and dry, how to limit activity, how to give any medications, and what to watch for. If you have questions after you get home, we’re a phone call away.</p>

faqs:
  - question: "Why is bloodwork recommended before surgery?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Pre-anesthetic bloodwork checks your pet’s overall health, including how the liver and kidneys are working, and helps show whether your pet is a good candidate for anesthesia. Minor issues may be corrected before surgery, and significant ones may mean postponing the procedure until they’re addressed.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/bloodwork">VCA Animal Hospitals, Preanesthetic Bloodwork</a></p>
  - question: "When should I spay or neuter my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>For cats, the American Veterinary Medical Association supports spaying or neutering by 5 months of age. For dogs there is no single best age — the right timing depends on breed, size, sex, lifestyle and health, so it’s a decision to make together with your veterinarian.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/spaying-and-neutering">American Veterinary Medical Association, Spaying and neutering</a></p>
  - question: "What are the health benefits of spaying or neutering?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Spaying females can prevent uterine infections and reduce the risk of breast cancer. Neutering males eliminates the risk of testicular cancer, lowers the risk of an enlarged prostate, and reduces roaming and urine marking. Both procedures also prevent unplanned litters.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/spaying-and-neutering">American Veterinary Medical Association, Spaying and neutering</a></p>
  - question: "How do I care for my pet at home after surgery?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Keep your pet indoors and quiet, with no running, jumping, stairs or rough play for the time your veterinarian recommends — often 7 to 14 days. Use leash walks for bathroom breaks, keep the incision dry, and use a cone (E-collar) or recovery suit so your pet can’t lick or chew the stitches.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/surgical-discharge-instructions-for-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Surgical Discharge Instructions for Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "When should I call after my pet’s surgery?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Check the incision every day. Mild redness and swelling are normal for the first few days and should slowly improve. Call your veterinary team if you see more than mild swelling, any discharge, open stitches, or if your pet’s condition changes or gets worse.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/surgical-discharge-instructions-for-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Surgical Discharge Instructions for Dogs</a></p>
