      <h2>Pet Biopsies Near Easley, SC</h2><p>Finding a new lump on your dog or cat, or a patch of skin that just won’t heal, can be worrying. Many lumps are harmless, but some are not — and there’s no reliable way to tell them apart just by looking or feeling. A biopsy gives you a real answer.</p><p>A biopsy is a procedure in which a small piece of tissue, or sometimes an entire lump, is removed so it can be examined under a microscope. At Clearview Animal Hospital, biopsies are one of the surgical services we offer. Samples are sent to a veterinary pathologist, a veterinarian who specializes in diagnosing disease from tissue. The results tell us what the problem is — such as an infection, an allergic or immune-related skin condition, or a benign or cancerous growth — so we can recommend the right treatment and help you understand what to expect.</p>

      <h2>What to Expect When Your Pet Has a Biopsy</h2><p>It starts with an exam. We’ll look at the lump or affected area, ask how long it has been there and whether it has changed, and check your pet’s overall health. In some cases, a fine needle aspirate — collecting a few cells with a small needle — is a helpful first step, and we’ll let you know if a biopsy is the better choice.</p><p>How the biopsy is done depends on where the area is and on your pet. Many skin biopsies only need a local anesthetic to numb the spot, while other pets need sedation or general anesthesia to stay comfortable and still. If anesthesia is recommended, pre-anesthetic bloodwork from our in-house laboratory helps confirm your pet is ready. We remove either a small section of tissue or the entire mass, and most small skin biopsy sites are closed with just a few stitches.</p><p>Afterward, we’ll explain how to care for the biopsy site at home, including keeping your pet from licking or scratching it. When the pathologist’s report comes back, we’ll go over the results with you and talk through the next steps.</p>

      <h2>Does Your Pet Need a Biopsy?</h2><p>We may recommend a biopsy for a lump or bump that appears suddenly or grows quickly, a growth that changes in size, color or texture, a sore or wound that won’t heal, skin that stays inflamed or irritated despite treatment, or a mass found during an exam, on X-rays or on ultrasound. If you notice something new on your pet, schedule an exam — it’s always better to check.</p><h2>When Should Your Pet Have a Biopsy?</h2><p>A biopsy isn’t a routine test — it’s done when there’s a specific area we need to understand. Sooner is usually better: a small lump is often easier to sample or remove than a large one, and knowing what it is lets us start the right treatment without delay. While you wait for results, keep an eye on the area and let us know about any changes.</p><h2>Practice Highlights</h2><p>Why families choose Clearview when their pet needs answers:</p><ul><li><strong>In-house laboratory</strong> — Pre-anesthetic bloodwork can be run right here at our hospital.</li><li><strong>Ultrasound and digital radiography</strong> — Imaging helps us look for masses inside the body and plan the next steps.</li><li><strong>Caring for Easley since 1958</strong> — A trusted local animal hospital for generations of Upstate pets.</li><li><strong>Payment options</strong> — CareCredit and Cherry can help you plan for the cost of care.</li></ul>

faqs:
  - question: "What can a biopsy tell us about my pet?"
    answer: |-
      <p>A biopsy shows what kind of cells make up an abnormal area of skin or a growth. It can tell the difference between infections, allergic skin disease, autoimmune skin disease and tumors, and whether a tumor is benign or malignant. That helps your veterinarian choose the most appropriate treatment and give you a sense of what to expect.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/biopsy-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Skin Biopsy in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "What’s the difference between a fine needle aspirate and a biopsy?"
    answer: |-
      <p>A fine needle aspiration uses a small needle to collect cells from a lump, while a biopsy removes a small amount of tissue from the growth. Both samples are examined under a microscope. Some tumors can be identified from an aspirate, while others need a biopsy to confirm the diagnosis.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/skin-disorders-of-dogs/tumors-of-the-skin-in-dogs">Merck Veterinary Manual, Tumors of the Skin in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "Will my pet need anesthesia for a biopsy?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Not always. In many cases the area is numbed with a local anesthetic. Depending on where the area is and on your pet’s temperament, a sedative or general anesthesia may be needed. Most skin biopsies are very small and need only a few stitches.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/biopsy-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Skin Biopsy in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "How long does it take to get biopsy results?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Biopsy samples are usually sent to a veterinary laboratory, where a board-certified veterinary pathologist examines them. Processing and evaluation typically take one to two weeks, though results can sometimes come back sooner. While you wait, watch your pet for new skin changes or changes to the biopsy area and report them to your veterinarian.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/biopsy-in-dogs">VCA Animal Hospitals, Skin Biopsy in Dogs</a></p>
  - question: "If a lump has already been removed, why is it still sent to a lab?"
    answer: |-
      <p>Examining the removed tissue under a microscope (histopathology) confirms exactly what type of tumor it was. For some tumors, such as mast cell tumors, the grade — which guides the outlook and whether more treatment is needed — can only be determined this way. Because these tumors can reach into nearby tissue, they’re removed with a margin of healthy tissue around them to help make sure all the cancerous cells are gone.</p><p class="faq-source">Source: <a href="https://www.acvs.org/small-animal/mast-cell-tumors">American College of Veterinary Surgeons, Mast Cell Tumors</a></p>
